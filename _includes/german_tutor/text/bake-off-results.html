An aside on the ceiling: it's not the phone's RAM spec that limits model size — it's iOS's per-app memory cap, which sits around 8 GB even on devices shipping 11–12 GB. I tried 12B-class models; they don't load. A ~5 GB 4-bit E4B with room for KV cache and the app itself is realistically the top of the on-device range today, which makes fine-tuning the only way up.

There is a capacity cliff, and fine-tuning below it actively harms. Gemma 3 1B was the shock: a respectable 58% baseline, and the identical fine-tune that lifted both 4B models dropped it to 32% — with false corrections exploding from 0% to 56%. My read: the dataset teaches a behavior (confidently produce FIX verdicts in a strict format), and a 1B model has room for the behavior but not the knowledge to aim it. It learned to sound like a tutor without knowing German well enough to be one. Below some capacity floor, fine-tuning doesn't add skill — it adds confidence, which is about the worst trade a teacher can make.

The base model's multilingual substrate sets the ceiling. The same data, recipe, and GPU gave E4B +13 points and Qwen3-4B +14 — a strikingly consistent lift. But tuned Qwen3-4B (62%) still landed below stock E4B (72%). You can't fine-tune your way past a weaker pretraining substrate with a dataset this size; you can only cash in the knowledge that's already latent. Pick the base by measured target-language quality, not by leaderboard rank.

Parameter count tells you almost nothing about German. Mistral 7B (43%) lost to Gemma 3 1B (58%), a model a tenth its size, and 8B Qwen matched 1B Gemma on the core suite. "Supports 100+ languages" on a model card is marketing; thirty minutes with a held-out eval is data. EuroLLM-1.7B made the same point from the other direction: pretrained on all 24 official EU languages, it scored 13% — not because it lacks German, but because it can't follow the correction-task format, answering in rambling English prose instead.

A reader later handed me the cleanest possible confirmation: BübleLM-2B, a Gemma 2-2B re-adapted for German with a custom German tokenizer and 3.5B tokens of German pretraining — a model built for exactly this. On its own German benchmarks it looks strong. On the app's task it scored 7%, below even EuroLLM: it never once said "OK", opened three-quarters of its answers with a bare reframed sentence and no verdict marker — and, tellingly, its actual German fixes were often correct. All the substrate, none of the discipline to deliver it in a parseable verdict. It's the whole thesis in one reader suggestion: the model card measures German; the eval measures the job.

The two sweeps that came later

BübleLM turned out to be the first of a cohort, not a one-off. Two more passes followed the original bake-off, months apart. One went hunting for anything that would fit a 4 GB phone. The other worked through the German-first specialists — models pretrained on German and little else, out of German and Swiss labs, each carrying benchmark numbers that look like they should win this outright. Same suites, same prompts, same scorer, with one difference that matters: by then the scorer had been repaired ("The eval was lying"), so every number below is scored the way the app actually renders a reply rather than the way the raw checkpoint answers. German pretraining buys fluency; it does not buy the job. Four German-first families, four independent pretraining corpora, 0.8 B to 7 B parameters — and the thing they all fail is not German. It's rendering a parseable verdict about someone else's sentence. Apertus-8B answers "The student's sentence is grammatically correct, but it can be improved…" with the correct fix buried inside the prose, and scores zero. Hand any of them three worked examples and format adherence jumps to 100% — which is a useful thing to know about a base and no help at all to an app that sends zero-shot prompts. The sharpest version of it is one number: on da-/wo-compounds, the phenomenon German learners struggle with most, every German-first model measured caps at 33% in its best condition, while stock Gemma 4 E2B — not German-first, merely well-trained multilingually — gets 60% zero-shot. Whatever produces da-/wo- competence, "more German pretraining data" is demonstrably not it.

The base that learns best is not the base that starts best. IBM's Granite models are the ones I did not see coming. They read German inefficiently — a 49k English-and-code vocabulary splits German words into fragments — and they start below every Gemma in this article. They are also the only bases in the survey that took the full corpus and got better for it: the 3.3 2B gained 22 items on the held-out suites, the first result in this whole project that clears an exact McNemar test (p = 0.002), and the newer 4.1 3B beat its own stock score by 53 and cut missed errors from 43% to 25%. Both now ship, to the 4 GB phones no Gemma tutor fits. Baseline German told me who was worth training; it did not tell me who would train well, and those turned out to be different questions.
