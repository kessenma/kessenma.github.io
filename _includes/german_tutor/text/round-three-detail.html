Gemma buys a huge multilingual vocabulary, which is exactly why it starts ahead on German and why the family dominates this entire article. But that table is not free, and on the shipped 4-bit weights you can measure the bill: 30% of Gemma 3 1B's file is vocabulary table, leaving a 0.39 GB body — and Gemma 4 E2B, which pays for its 262k vocabulary a second time through per-layer embeddings, spends 1.55 of its 3.55 GB on embeddings alone. Granite's bet is the mirror image: 4% of Granite 3.3 2B is vocabulary, 96% is body. Granite 4.1 3B carries a bigger body in a 1.9 GB download than Gemma 4 E2B carries in 3.55 GB.

That asymmetry is the mechanism behind "room to grow," and it isn't a metaphor. A vocabulary table is knowledge that arrives fully formed and barely moves: it fixes what the model can read, and the LoRA adapters in this project never touch it — they attach to the attention and feed-forward projections, which all live in the body. The body is the only place new behavior can go. So Gemma 3 1B walks in knowing more German than anything else its size and with almost nowhere to put a correction habit — train it and the judgment gets crowded out. That's the collapse, and it's why 28× the data made it worse. Granite walks in knowing much less German and with a body six times larger standing empty. It learns: +17 points from the same corpus that broke the 1B.

What Granite 3.3 couldn't do was read German efficiently. A 49k vocabulary trained mostly on English and code stores German in pieces — gelesen arrives as ge+les+en. Across all 203 eval sentences that costs 1.43× the tokens Gemma needs for the same text, which is ~40% slower generation on the weakest hardware in the lineup and ~30% less effective context. (A third consequence — body capacity spent reassembling word fragments instead of doing grammar, capping the ceiling — fits the data, but I can't measure it, so it stays a hypothesis.) A model that knows German but can't learn, or a model that can learn but reads German in pieces: under 4 GB those were the only two options on offer, and the ceiling sat at 72% either way. I paused the tier there.

The obvious fix — a small model with Gemma's vocabulary and Granite's body ratio — didn't exist, and the reason is economic rather than technical. German is ~2% of the world's speakers; frontier labs optimize benchmark averages across many languages, and multilingual capacity at small sizes is bought with vocabulary and data mix, both of which cost English performance. A German-first 1B is a rounding error in every market a lab measures. TII's Falcon models are the clean illustration: a well-funded lab producing genuinely capable open models, aimed squarely at Arabic and English — because that's who funded them. Nobody is wrong here, and nothing in the incentives points at German. What exists instead is community and academic work (BübleLM, SauerkrautLM, Salamandra, EuroLLM), and the eval already told that story: they lift general German fluency and lose the instruction discipline that makes a verdict format work at all.

What lifted the pause was the teacher, not the tier

The thread left dangling two sections ago — relative pronouns regressing identically on two unrelated architectures — was the visible end of a bigger problem, and chasing it is what eventually cracked this tier. The v2 corpus had two defects. Its verdict mix was never checked per phenomenon, so four of five separable-verb examples said nothing was wrong and every model trained on it drifted toward silence. And the teacher that generated the hard phenomena was a 26B mixture-of-experts with only ~4B parameters active per token — which is to say I had been running the students' own capacity limit one level up, and grading the results with gates that only asked whether data was present. Rebuilding with a dense 31B teacher moved the reflexive case-change rate from 12% to 98% and cut mislabeled separable-verb "errors" from 26% to 2%. Balancing verdicts per phenomenon at pack time did the rest.

That corpus — call it v4 — went through four bases in one two-day sweep, recipe held constant, so every row below differs only in what the base brought. Total GPU spend for all four: about $10.

Read the last two columns against the vocabulary share and the whole argument of this section is sitting in one table. The identical corpus cost Gemma 4 E2B eight items — and the losses landed on the extension suite, phenomena it had never been trained on either way, so the added breadth interfered with competence it already had rather than extending it. The same corpus moved Granite 3.3 by +22 items, an exact McNemar p = 0.002 and the first statistically significant paired win this project produced in four training campaigns. And it moved Granite 4.1 by +53 items over its own stock score. Nothing about the data changed between those rows. What changed was how much unspoken-for body each student had to put it in.

Granite 4.1 3B is the model that finally threads the needle, and it does it on exactly the axis this section is about: 100,352 tokens of vocabulary against 3.3's 49k — still only 8% of its weights, but enough to read German at 1.15× Gemma's token cost instead of 1.43×, which is most of the tokenizer tax gone. So the phone that used to get a model showing the learner nothing on every single real error now gets 164/203 at a 25% miss rate, in a 1.9 GB file. Both Granites are listed in the app; which one stays is decided by peak resident memory on real 4 GB hardware, where 1.9 GB of weights clears the floor but sits close enough to the app's headroom fraction that the KV cache would get capped. That measurement is the last open item on this tier.

Two honest caveats travel with the result. Granite 4.1 buys its error-catching with judgment — 9% false corrections against Granite 3.3's 0% — which is the knowledge-versus-judgment trade of this whole article showing up one tier further down. And 164 is still seven items short of what a 6 GB phone already gets. But the shape of the claim has changed. "Below ~4B effective parameters, judgment gets crowded out" was the right shape and the wrong number: it was measured on models that spend a third to a half of their weights on a vocabulary table. Restated in body terms it holds and it is more useful — below ~2B of body a corpus can only re-style a model, at ~2.5B it gets absorbed but verdict courage lags, and at ~3B of dense body the knowledge / judgment / format trade finally resolves. That is a statement about how to spend a parameter budget, not about how new the phone has to be. Every result here is still a statement about one generation of small models — but this time the next generation didn't have to arrive. Something already on the shelf, that I had written off for reading German badly, turned out to be the one with room left to teach.
