Over the last year I've tinkered with a handful of different libraries to run small models on phones: Cactus Compute, ExecuTorch, llama.cpp, ONNX Runtime Mobile, and most recently Apple's MLX. These libraries are all quite similar. They have different formats, and some optimize for performance on lower-end hardware (Cactus) while others focus on being as cross-compatible as possible (llama.cpp with the .gguf format), but at the end of the day they're all running models between 0.5B and ~8B parameters. Google's Gemma, Alibaba's Qwen, and Meta's Llama models have been the most popular with these libraries lately.

All these baseline models are decent at creating flashcards in German with conjugation tables, but… I wanted to push the boundaries and see if these models could be used for a more interactive learning experience, with a hero-feature of being able to have turn-based voice conversations in German with a LLM. All the baseline models technically can converse in German, but … they didn't seem to follow all the grammar rules I was being taught in my German classes, or they would form simple sentences lacking complexity. So I wanted to experiment with Unsloth, an open-source fine-tuning framework for LLM's to mold the available open-source models. Surprisingly, the "German-first" models are worse in my testing at catching German grammar mistakes. Then another complication I reached with many of the german-first models is their restricted licenses. Google Gemma's and IBM's Granite models are both licensed under the Apache 2.0 license, and I found them to be the best models for my use case.

This article is the write-up of those experiments: the eval, the dataset, the training runs, a survey of every phone-sized model I could load — and the two times my own scoring pipeline lied to me. Every acronym in here (QLoRA, MLX, 4-bit…) is unpacked in the glossary at the end — the first mention of each is a dotted link straight to its definition.

The app, briefly

The app started out as a simple flashcard app: download a model from HuggingFace, run it on my phone, have it generate decks on prompt-generated topics. It worked well enough — but my peers in class had family, partners, and more German-speaking friends than me, and I craved more German conversations. That became the hero feature: a turn-based voice conversation with the model, with the trimmings I wished other apps had — translating phrases in either direction mid-conversation, saving words I didn't know straight into a flashcard deck, and real-time correction of my German. Around it grew the rest of a study routine, shaped by the A2/B1 practice exams and by life as an American job-hunting in Vienna: reading-and-listening stories with comprehension quizzes, grammar drills, a vocab matching game, a saved-phrases inbox for things overheard in the wild, a job-interview role-play mode, a photo → vocab feature, and a learner profile the model quietly maintains about my strengths and weaknesses.
