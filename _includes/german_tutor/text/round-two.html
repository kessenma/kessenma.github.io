Round two: taking the recipe down-market

With the E4B tutor shipped, the project picked up an explicit second goal: make older and cheaper phones a first-class target instead of a downgrade. A ~5 GB model wants roughly 8 GB of RAM; everything below that was still stuck with a stock model. What I wanted was a capable tutor in the 1–3B-parameter class — and the bake-off had flagged Gemma 4 E2B (5.1B raw parameters, ~2B "effective" via the E-series' per-layer-embedding trick) as the one candidate worth training: 65% base on the core four, and the exact same over-correction personality as its big sibling (34% false corrections) — precisely the failure the dataset was built to fix. Same data, same QLoRA recipe, one line changed to swap the base model. On paper a fifteen-minute job. It was not.

This time nothing in RunPod's cheap 24 GB tier was available, so the run landed on an RTX 4000 Ada (20 GB) at $0.23/hr — plenty of VRAM, but a barer image whose externally-managed system Python refused pip install unsloth outright (PEP 668) until --break-system-packages.

The real friction was an out-of-memory kill that had nothing to do with the GPU. The run died at 91% of the tokenizing pass with a single word — Killed — and no traceback, while the GPU sat idle at 8 of its 20 GB. This was the kernel's OOM killer. The pod advertised 48 vCPUs and free reported 251 GB of RAM — but that 251 GB is the host's. The container's actual cgroup limit was 28.9 GB with zero swap. Unsloth's tokenizer, like most of the Hugging Face data stack, scales its worker count to the visible CPUs, so it forked 52 parallel workers and sailed straight past 29 GB into an instant SIGKILL. The fix is one line — pin dataset_num_proc=2 — and RAM dropped to a steady 12 GB. The general lesson for rented GPUs: many vCPUs paired with a small RAM cap is the dangerous combination, so read the container's cgroup limit and never trust free. The most time-consuming problem of the entire E2B run was infrastructure — not the model, not the data, not one line of German.

Past that wall the run was uneventful: the same 1,447 examples, ~26 minutes on the slower Ada card against the 3090's 19 for E4B, held-out eval loss flat around 0.75 (E4B: 0.67) — a hair higher, as you'd expect from a smaller model, and just as stable. Then the scorecard came back looking like a disaster. Core accuracy nudged up (65% → 68%), and the model got measurably better at catching real errors (miss rate 28% → 19%) — but false corrections nearly doubled, 34% → 59%, complete with confidently invented grammar rules: Sie wartet auf ihren Freund (perfect) came back "corrected" to the identical sentence with the note "'warten auf' is inseparable; no 'zu' is needed." It looked exactly like the 1B's capacity cliff one tier up — knowledge absorbed, judgment crowded out. I benched the checkpoint, wrote "round two" off as a negative result, and started planning a rescue: gentler recipes, a data remix, maybe DPO.

Then I looked closer at what those "false corrections" actually were — and the story fell apart in the best possible way.
