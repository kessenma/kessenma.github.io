Glossary — the jargon, unpacked

Everything above in plain terms, roughly in the order it shows up.

MLX
Apple's machine-learning framework, built for the unified memory of Apple silicon — the CPU and GPU share one pool of RAM, so a model doesn't have to be copied back and forth. It's what makes running a ~5 GB language model on an iPhone practical at all. mlx-lm is its language-model toolkit (loading, generating, converting); MLX Swift is the Swift binding my app actually ships. Think of it as Apple's answer to PyTorch, tuned for phones and Macs instead of data-center GPUs.
Quantization / "4-bit"
A model's weights are normally 16-bit numbers. Quantization rounds them to fewer bits — 4, in my case — which shrinks the file roughly 4× and speeds up inference, at a small cost in quality. It's the single reason a phone-sized model exists: Gemma 4 E4B is ~16 GB at full precision and ~4.9 GB at 4-bit. Every number in this article was measured on the quantized model, because that's what actually runs on the phone — measuring the full-precision version would be measuring something I don't ship.
LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)
Fine-tuning a whole model means updating billions of weights — expensive, and you end up with a whole new multi-gigabyte model. LoRA instead freezes the original model and trains a small pair of "adapter" matrices alongside it, learning only the change you want. The adapter is a few megabytes. The rank (r=8 here) is how much capacity the adapter has: higher rank learns more, but overfits faster on a small dataset. The adapter can be merged back into the base weights afterward, which is what ships.
QLoRA
LoRA, but with the frozen base model held in memory in quantized form while you train. That's the whole trick: quantizing the frozen part slashes the VRAM needed, so a fine-tune that would otherwise demand a data-center card fits on a single rented consumer GPU. It's why this project's training bill was measured in cents rather than hundreds of dollars. The output is still a small LoRA adapter.
Unsloth
An open-source library that wraps Hugging Face's training stack with hand-optimized kernels, making QLoRA fine-tuning roughly 2× faster and substantially more memory-efficient. It also ships ready-made recipes per model family, which is how a 19-minute training run on a rented GPU is even a sentence you can write.
Catastrophic forgetting
When you fine-tune a model hard on one narrow task, it can lose abilities it used to have — it "forgets" everything that isn't the new task. The standard defense is to blend general-purpose examples into the training mix so the model keeps practicing its old skills. That's what the 516 general-German examples (~35% of the set) are for, and the extension suite going up (87% → 90%) rather than down is the evidence it worked.
Held-out eval
A test set the model never sees during training. If an eval sentence leaks into the training data, the model can memorize the answer and the score becomes a lie — which is why the validation gate explicitly rejects any generated example that resembles an eval item. Mine are two suites: core (60 items in the four target areas) and extension (61 items in ten areas I deliberately did not train on, to detect catastrophic forgetting).
Teacher model
A big, capable model used to generate training data for a smaller one — the small model learns from the big one's output. The catch this project ran into: the student inherits the teacher's mistakes, faithfully. A cheap teacher that mislabels 42% of its "this sentence is correct" examples doesn't produce a slightly worse student; it produces a student trained to make exactly the error you were trying to eliminate.
LanguageTool
An open-source grammar checker (the engine behind a lot of "proofread this" features). I self-hosted it as an automated gate on generated data. Its German rules are strong on spelling, agreement and punctuation — and, as I found out, caught none of my four target error types. Useful as a floor, useless as a ceiling.
spaCy
An industrial NLP library. I used its German model for structural checks the grammar checker can't do: parsing a sentence into its parts of speech and dependency tree, so a script can ask "is the verb actually separable here?" or "does this sentence really contain a reflexive pronoun in the dative?" It's also what mined the 83,000 seed sentences out of Tatoeba's 774k.
Tatoeba
A large, free, community-built corpus of example sentences with translations — 774,000 of them in German. Crucially, they're natural sentences written by humans, which makes them good seeds: I mined them for real usages of my target constructions, then built synthetic learner errors from there rather than inventing sentences from nothing.
CEFR level (A1–C2)
The European scale for language proficiency: A1/A2 beginner, B1/B2 intermediate, C1/C2 advanced. The app tracks yours so the tutor can pitch its German at you, and every training example carries a CEFR tag so the model learns to answer a B1 learner differently from a C1 one.
Chat template
The exact text format a chat model expects — special tokens marking where each turn starts and who's speaking. Every family has its own, and they're strict: Gemma 4's requires that turns strictly alternate user → model → user, which is why my tutor dialogues (where the model greets you first) crashed training until I gave them an opening user turn. If your training data's format doesn't match what the model sees in production, you're training on a token stream that will never occur.
KV cache
As a model generates, it caches intermediate values ("keys" and "values") for every token so far, so it doesn't recompute the whole conversation for each new word. It's what makes generation fast — and it grows with conversation length, eating RAM the app also needs. On a phone with a hard per-app memory cap, the KV cache is why you can't simply fill the budget with model weights.
safetensors
The standard file format for model weights (a safer replacement for Python pickles, which could execute code on load). Relevant here for an unglamorous reason: MLX Swift merges every .safetensors file it finds in a model folder, so leaving a stray LoRA adapter in the repo silently corrupts loading.
Raw vs. guarded ("app-equivalent") scoring
Two ways to score the same saved model responses. Raw takes the model's text at face value. Guarded first runs it through the same parsing and filtering the app applies before showing anything to a learner — most importantly discarding "echo" corrections, where the model wraps the student's own sentence unchanged in a FIX: header. Raw measures the checkpoint; guarded measures what a user experiences. Mixing the two scales is how this project fooled itself, twice.
Modal particles
The little flavoring words — doch, mal, eigentlich, ja, halt, eben, schon — that carry tone rather than meaning and make spoken German sound like a person instead of a textbook. ("Komm her" is an order; "Komm mal her" is an invitation.) Their frequency per 100 tokens turned out to be the single clearest measurable marker of the "sounds AI generated" complaint, which is why the naturalness bench tracks it.
Dev-set drift
A held-out test set stays honest only as long as you don't act on it. Score twenty models against the same suite, make ship decisions from the results, iterate — and you've quietly been selecting for whatever does well on those particular items. The numbers stay reproducible while becoming optimistic. The fix is a fresh frozen holdout you score once and never tune against; on one of those, the shipped E4B's lead over E2B mostly evaporated.
Mixture of experts (MoE)
A model architecture where each token activates only a fraction of the total weights — the v2 data campaign's 26B teacher runs just ~4B "active" parameters per token. You pay for the full model's memory but generate at close to a small model's speed, which is what made 61,000 teacher generations cost about $6.
Per-layer embeddings (PLE)
The architectural trick behind Gemma 4's E-series: much of the model's bulk lives in per-layer embedding tables that can stream from storage, so a 5.1B-parameter model behaves like ~2B in RAM. The catch discovered here: those embeddings are extremely sensitive to quantization and must stay high-precision — which is why no real 3-bit version of these models can exist (the protected part dominates the average bits-per-weight).
McNemar test
A statistical test for comparing two models on the same test items, looking only at the items where they disagree. On an 82-item suite, one model scoring 67% and another 72% — 9 items gained, 5 lost — gives p = 0.42: entirely consistent with a coin flip. It's the honest way to admit that a small benchmark can rank models loosely but can't certify a 5-point gap.
Tokenizer (BPE vs. SentencePiece)
The fixed dictionary that chops text into the units a model actually reads. A big multilingual vocabulary (Gemma's 262k SentencePiece) stores whole German words as single tokens; a small, English-and-code-heavy one (Granite's 49k byte-level BPE) has to spell German out in fragments — same sentence, ~1.9× the tokens. That taxes generation speed and context length forever, and no amount of fine-tuning changes it: the tokenizer is decided the day the model is pretrained.
Knowledge distillation
Training a small "student" model to match a big teacher's full output distribution (its probabilities over every token), not just its final text — a richer signal than ordinary fine-tuning. On-policy distillation was the planned last lever for the 4 GB tier, and it died on a technicality: teacher and student must share a vocabulary for their token probabilities to even line up, and Gemma and Granite don't.
